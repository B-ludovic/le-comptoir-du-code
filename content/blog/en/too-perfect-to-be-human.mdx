There's one detail that never lies when I scroll through professional social networks: the writing has become far too perfect.

## Creative chaos and the void beneath the syntax

The human mind is chaotic by nature. When someone has a big idea, their thinking never comes out as an immaculate bulleted list. One thought leads to another, a chapter opens onto a new problem, and the final idea builds itself out of that creative disorder. That controlled chaos is what gives a text a soul, because it reflects the path of an actual brain at work.

Let's be precise: a good human writer can produce clean copy. But clean and interchangeable are not the same thing. Today, the overwhelming majority of posts wear the same sterile structure—a formatted hook, three smooth paragraphs, an obvious conclusion—and could be signed by anyone. Nothing sticks out, nothing snags, nothing was risked. What you're reading is the endless cloning of ideas seen a hundred times, where the template did the thinking instead of the author. It's not writing anymore; it's filling in a template.

I'm the first to work with AI, and I make no secret of it. But I know what I ask of it. Artificial intelligence is an exoskeleton, not a prosthesis for a missing thought. What I publish comes entirely out of my own creative mind. The tool helps me adjust; the raw material is mine.

## The empty machine

All this synthetic theater leads to an uncomfortable truth: what these platforms promise—connections, opportunities—is wildly out of proportion with what they actually deliver.

Millions of professionals are posting frantically to feed an algorithm that gives almost nothing back. We applaud viral posts—sometimes funny the first time—that immediately spawn a dozen bland variants. And we mistake fleeting visibility for the building of professional trust. I did end up forming one genuine connection this way. One. After how much time showing up, and how many words thrown into the void? The ratio is the real verdict: it's not that the machine produces nothing, it's that it demands everything to produce almost nothing.

## The comfort of the half-empty glass

You might say I'm seeing the glass half empty. That's true, and I fully own it. Seeing this microcosm for what it is—a machine where we toss artificial words into the void to beg for attention—protects me from disappointment.

Real professional connection isn't forged in the smooth perfection of a post generated in ten seconds. It's forged in the exchange of real skills, in hard work, and in the authenticity of our flaws.

Let the algorithm spin in the void. We have real projects to build.
