People who want to launch an online business often have very good ideas. What interests me is how they see their own project: as a halo. Warm and smooth. Nothing more to say about it—it speaks for itself.

Where they see a halo, I see a spider web. Threads everywhere, knots, and a few spots that snap if you pull on them. The collision between the two images always happens at the same moment: the MVP presentation. And I'm not saying this to sound interesting—I had exactly the same halo when I started M.U.S.E., and it cost me dearly.

## The MVP the machine spat out

More and more often, someone brings me a spec whose origin is beyond doubt by the second line. I read it all the way through, politely. And I realize there's nothing in it. Not nothing as in "it's incomplete": nothing as in not a single decision has been made.

Let me be upfront: I say this as a heavy user of these tools. I work with Claude every day, openly. The problem isn't that AI stays quiet about the hard topics—ask it to spec an online store and it will probably mention GDPR and the withdrawal period somewhere in its three pages of response. The problem is that in those three pages, nothing distinguishes the decorative line from the one that can cost you your business. Sorting one from the other takes a frame of reference: having already seen what breaks, and what it costs when it does. Which is precisely what a founder doesn't have yet.

Describe to the AI the journey of a customer who checks out without a hitch, and it will hand you a lovely paragraph. The customer who gets charged twice, the one who's fifteen, the one who demands a refund on day thirteen: those, you have to know they exist to go looking for them.

> Is it my job, as a developer, to design the operational core of their business? Yes. That's exactly what I get paid for. A store that takes people's money demands a different kind of rigor than a conversation with Claude—and that rigor is the product.

## The blind spot, then the wall

Dig a little and the varnish comes off fast. That's when I pull on the threads of the web, one by one. How does the product actually get to the customer? Who receives the money, at what moment, and what happens if the payment fails halfway through? How long does the database hold before it needs rethinking? These aren't the questions of a nitpicking developer. They're the ones that determine whether you have a business or not—and every thread that snaps between my fingers during that conversation is a thread that would have snapped in production, with real customers on it.

Then comes the wall. That one I actually like, because it doesn't negotiate.

Minors, for instance: nothing in the terms of sale about what happens when the buyer isn't old enough to enter a contract. Membership in a consumer mediation scheme—mandatory in France as soon as you sell to individuals—never mentioned. GDPR reduced to a checkbox. The withdrawal period treated as a UI detail. Those lines may well have been somewhere in the generated answer—drowned between two paragraphs, at the same typographic level as the choice of color palette. Nothing signaled that the wall was there.

## The halo is not the problem

Let me be clear: the halo isn't a naivety to be cured. It's the normal state of any idea before it meets reality—mine included. Without it, nobody would ever start anything, and it's what provides the energy to get through everything else. My job isn't to switch it off. My job is to weave the web underneath, so the idea holds the day someone pulls on it.

A good idea is the starting point, and that's already a lot. But between the idea and the product, there's the plumbing: pipes to connect, ugly edge cases to handle, obligations to absorb. That doesn't get generated. It gets assembled by hand, by someone who has already watched the water leak.
