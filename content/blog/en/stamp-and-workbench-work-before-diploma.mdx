Ever since I dove into development, one regret has stuck with me: not starting sooner. Creating, designing architectures, working through blockers and shipping a tool that works—there's a satisfaction in it that eighteen years of management never gave me in quite this form: the craftsman's satisfaction of tidying the workbench at night and seeing something that didn't exist in the morning.

Today I'm preparing the Concepteur Développeur d'Applications title—a French state-recognized developer certification. The first two blocks are already validated; the rest is underway. And along the way, one question keeps imposing itself: why does the professional world still demand a piece of paper when the finished work is right there—browsable, clickable?

## Eloquence versus material

The hiring process measures what it knows how to measure: the interview. The oral performance, the ease, the storytelling. So it mechanically rewards those who tell the story well—and that's a real skill, I don't look down on it. But I managed teams of twelve to fifteen people for eighteen years in airport retail, and if there's one thing that job teaches you, it's how to tell apart the person who narrates the work from the person who does it. Both exist on every team. The first one shines in meetings. The second one, you discover by looking at the shop floor.

My shop floor, these days, is the workbench. Not that speaking is foreign to me—you don't spend eighteen years behind a counter without learning how to talk to people—but because I refuse to let the pitch stand in for the deliverable. Where the interview asks you to explain what you could do, I'd rather sit down and build what needs to exist.

My latest delivered project is exactly that. Fairy Chair Studio: the online store of an afro hair salon, carried solo from end to end. Needs analysis, architecture, real-time stock, Stripe payments with partial and full refunds, Colissimo shipping exports, PDF invoicing, legal compliance. Every hat, including the ones that itch—the terms of sale, the edge cases, the customer who cancels half an order. I wish my client the success she deserves; the rest, the store says better than I can.

## The "fake junior" paradox

On an HR grid, a career changer without an official title is immediately labeled "junior". The label says something true—two years of code, not ten—but it flattens everything else: eighteen years of schedules, inventories, conflicts to defuse and airport-terminal surprises, with a team to keep running no matter what. Professional maturity, work ethic, the ability to carry a product from the need to the delivery: none of that is learned in a textbook, and none of it vanished the day I changed careers.

That's the fake junior paradox: junior on paper, senior at the workbench. Code is learned fast when you already know how to work. The reverse is far less true.

## Why sit for the title anyway?

If the work speaks, why pay the toll at all?

Because in France, the RNCP title—the state registry of professional certifications—remains a tollgate: it proves nothing to people in the trade, it reassures recruitment algorithms and administrative grids. And because the work only speaks to those who know how to read it. A developer who opens the store's code sees the security of the payment flows and the handling of edge cases. A CV-screening tool opens nothing at all: it looks for one line, a single one, and without it the file leaves the pile before ever being read. The title is the sworn translation—the same craft, converted into the only language those systems can decipher.

So I'm not preparing this title to discover my craft. I'm preparing it to align the administrative label with the reality of the workbench.

The day it arrives, the stamp will go in a frame, somewhere on the workshop wall. The workbench will already have moved on to the next project.
