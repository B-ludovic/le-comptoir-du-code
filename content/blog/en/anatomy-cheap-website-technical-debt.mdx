If you post an ad for a new website on a freelance marketplace, you'll receive dozens of replies within minutes. Among them, you'll find quotes that defy all economic logic: complete, "turnkey" websites for €300 or €500.

It's not magic, and it's generally not a scam. It's simply assembly-line production.

As a developer, I am regularly contacted to "fix" or upgrade these types of platforms a few months after they go live. Here is exactly what I find when I open the hood.

## 1. The Frankenstein Syndrome (The 45 Plugins)

To be profitable at €300, a contractor cannot afford to spend time coding. The standard method therefore consists of installing a CMS (usually WordPress), slapping on a heavy visual theme, and adding a third-party plugin for every single requested feature.

- A plugin for the contact form.
- A plugin to speed up the site (which is only slow because of the theme).
- A plugin for security.
- A plugin to link your social networks.

The result 6 months later: your website is a house of cards. At the first major core or database update, one of these plugins becomes incompatible. A white screen of death appears, or worse, a security vulnerability opens up. Meanwhile, the original contractor has already moved on to their next 20 projects.

## 2. "Generated" Code (The Visual Illusion)

Today, there are tools that allow you to design a mockup (in Figma, for example) and automatically export the HTML/CSS code with the click of a button. This is often the secret weapon behind express quotes.

Visually, on your screen, the result meets your expectations.

Technically, it's a disaster. Where a software artisan would write 50 lines of well-structured code to display a section, an automatic export generates 800 lines of spaghetti code, using absolute positioning without any semantic hierarchy.

The result 6 months later: Google hates bloated, non-semantic code. Your SEO never takes off. Even worse, the day you simply want to change a text alignment or adapt the layout for a new mobile screen size, the entire structure collapses.

## 3. The Technical Debt Wall

This is the most critical point. Let's assume your business is doing well. Six months after launching your €300 site, you want to add a custom booking system or a secure client portal.

You contact a professional developer for this upgrade. They open the source code, analyze the architecture, heave a sigh, and deliver the classic verdict of our industry:

> "The code is so tangled and unstable that trying to add a new feature will break everything. It will actually be cheaper to rebuild the whole thing from scratch."

This is what we call **technical debt**. You thought you got a great deal at launch, but you end up paying twice for the development of your business tool.

## The Verdict: Prototype vs. Foundation

A quote doesn't just define a price. It defines a methodology.

A €300 website is a **prototype**. If your goal is simply to have a URL to put on a business card for a month to test a brand name, it's a perfectly rational choice.

But if this website is meant to generate revenue, capture leads, automate your processes, or last for several years, you need **foundations**.

Building foundations requires engineering:

- Architecting the database before writing the first line of code.
- Targeting absolute performance (using Next.js and modern hosting).
- Delivering clean, documented source code that can be easily picked up by any other developer 3 years from now.

The real question isn't how much your website costs today, but how much it will cost you the day you need it to grow.
